Abstract

ORDA models the process by which vetted researchers obtain access to platform data under Article 40 of the EU Digital Services Act (Regulation 2022/2065), for the purpose of studying systemic risks. It covers the actors involved, the request lifecycle, the data made available, and the safeguards attached to it.

The ontology's organising distinction is between the two access routes that Article 40 creates: the Art. 40(4) route, in which a request for non-public data is mediated by a Digital Services Coordinator and results in a reasoned request to the platform, and the Art. 40(12) route, in which publicly accessible data is requested directly from the platform without intermediation. These are modelled as disjoint classes because they differ in who may apply, who assesses the application, and what the platform is obliged to provide.

3. Introduction

Article 40 of the Digital Services Act obliges providers of Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) and Very Large Online Search Engines (VLOSEs) to give researchers access to data for studying systemic risks in the Union. Delegated Regulation 2025/2050 sets out the procedure for the non-public data route. The process spans several actors — researchers and their institutions, the Digital Services Coordinator of establishment, the coordinator of the researcher's own member state, and the platform — and moves through a sequence of assessments with defined outcomes.

ORDA gives that process a machine-readable vocabulary. It types the requests, the parties, the assessments and their statuses, the data catalogues platforms must publish, and the technical, legal and organisational safeguards attached to an access modality. It reuses established vocabularies wherever one already covers the ground — 13 of them, listed in section 12 — and defines new terms only for what is specific to the Article 40 regime.

This draft scopes the model to the application stage: submitting a request, assessing it, and issuing the reasoned request that obliges the platform to supply data. Decision records, procedural deadlines, dispute settlement and research outputs are named in the legislation but are not yet modelled; section 14 lists them.

5.1 Axiomatic commitments

The vocabulary is axiomatised rather than left as a bare taxonomy, so that a description-logic reasoner can detect malformed data. Four kinds of axiom carry most of that weight, and each term entry below records the ones that apply to it.

5.2 Deliberate non-disjointness

Three overlaps are intentional and should not be "fixed" by adding disjointness axioms.

  • orda:DSCofEstablishment and orda:DSCofMemberState — where the researcher's institution and the platform are established in the same member state, one authority holds both roles.
  • orda:LegalModality, orda:OrganisationalModality and orda:TechnicalModality — under DA Art. 9 modalities combine. A secure enclave governed by a confidentiality undertaking is both technical and legal, and disjointness would force it to be split into two unrelated individuals.
  • orda:ApplicantResearcher and orda:VettedResearcher — a researcher named on an application may already hold vetted status. orda:PrincipalResearcher is a sub-class of the former, so the principal researcher is always among the applicants.

13. Example Data

Illustrative instance data is distributed separately, in orda-examples.ttl: a worked Art. 40(4) application from a university researcher to a designated platform, with its status history, funding chain, and the CSV, JSON and PDF distributions returned.

It is kept out of the vocabulary file deliberately. Those individuals are described by ORDA rather than defined by it, so they carry no rdfs:isDefinedBy assertion and do not appear as terms in this document. The example file declares its own ontology IRI, https://example.org/orda/examples, and imports ORDA.

The example data satisfies every class restriction in this document concretely rather than vacuously: the worked application names a principal researcher who is affiliated with a university, the project names both its grant and the agency funding that grant, and the application carries the Art. 40(8)(g) publication commitment.

14. Conformance Notes

Two limitations are worth stating plainly for anyone reusing this vocabulary.

Datatype profile. Six datatype properties range over xsd:date, which is not part of the OWL 2 datatype map. Reasoners that enforce the map — HermiT among them — will refuse to load the ontology until those are changed to xsd:dateTime or a custom datatype is declared. With that substitution the ontology and its example data are consistent and contain no unsatisfiable classes.

Imported-term narrowing. ORDA asserts domains, ranges and restrictions on terms it does not define — including dct:format, dct:hasFormat, dct:identifier, several frapo properties, frapo:Funding and several dpv properties. These axioms propagate to anyone importing ORDA, and are stronger than the source vocabularies assert. Importers who need the unconstrained terms should import the source vocabulary directly.

Scoped out of this draft, and named here so their absence is not mistaken for an oversight: assessment decision records and rejection grounds; the procedural deadlines in DA Art. 7, Art. 40(5) and DA Art. 13; dispute settlement bodies; research outputs and publication records beyond the commitment flag; and the linkage between a dcat:Distribution and the ddp:ActivityHistory structures it serialises.
